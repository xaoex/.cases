- **Legal Basis:** Common law doctrine

## 6. Legal Arguments

### 6.1 Primary Arguments

1. **Substantial Performance Doctrine:** Party A substantially performed contract obligations. The software met all material specifications. Under the substantial performance doctrine (recognized in Smith v. Jones), Party A is entitled to payment minus any reasonable deduction for minor deficiencies.

2. **Material Breach by Party B:** Party B's refusal to pay constitutes material breach. The contract provided for payment upon delivery, with a quality assurance period. Party B cannot unilaterally refuse payment without following dispute resolution procedures outlined in contract.

3. **Waiver and Acceptance:** Party B's continued use of the software for over 4 months constitutes acceptance and waiver of any defect claims. Under Example State Contract Act § 123.67, acceptance cannot be revoked after reasonable period of use.

### 6.2 Counterarguments (Anticipated Defense)

1. **Expected defense:** "Product did not meet specifications, late delivery"
   - **Response:** Expert testimony confirms 95% specification compliance. Any defects were minor and corrected. Late delivery was only 14 days with valid reasons (documented in DOC-003a). Contract penalty for late delivery is €500, which can be deducted from payment, but does not excuse payment entirely.

2. **Expected defense:** "Software has defects making it unusable"
   - **Response:** Usage logs (DOC-007) prove Party B has been actively using software for commercial purposes for over 4 months. This demonstrates software is not only usable but valuable to their business operations.
